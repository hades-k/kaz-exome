Material and methods2.1	Sample collection and OverviewThe full dataset comprises 125 whole exome sequenced (WES) samples from Kazakh individuals. The samples have been collected, sequenced using Illumina (HiSeq ?) and preprocessed in 2014 (?). Due to the time elapsed and lack of direct involvement in the initial collection, specific details about sample preparation, DNA extraction protocols, raw data quality control, or specific variant calling steps are not available.?  2.2	Quality control and Preprocessing of Raw Sequencing ReadsTo better understand the variant discovery process and gain hands-on experience with bioinformatics workflows, I selected two representative samples (WE001 and WE002) and performed the variant calling pipeline on them. This included quality control, alignment, duplicate marking, base recalibration, variant calling, joint genotyping, filtering, and annotation. Although this subset is not used in downstream population-level analysis, it served as a technical familiarization exercise and a basis for comparison with the preprocessed variant calls provided in the full dataset.2.2.1 Initial Quality Assessment with FastQCThe first step in processing of the raw sequencing data was quality assessment using FastQC [1], a quality control tool for high-throughput sequence data. FastQC provides metrics such as per-base sequence quality, GC content, presence of adapter sequences, and duplication levels, which help identify potential issues before further analysis. The assessment was performed on WE001 and WE002, for both forward (R1) and reverse (R2) strands. These paired-end reads, generated by an Illumina platform, typically range in quality, with degradation often observed toward the 3’ ends of reads. [publication?]The quality reports showed an overall acceptable quality, but with mild 3’ end score drop-off. Although no adapter sequences were detected, quality trimming was deemed appropriate.2.2.2 Quality Trimming with Trimmomatic To clean and improve the quality of the raw reads, trimming was performed using Trimmomatic [2], a tool for Illumina NGS data preprocessing. Trimmomatic was selected due to its compatibility with paired-end reads, flexibility, and multithread performance.The trimming process uses three key algorithms:-	Leading/Trailing: these modules scan each read from the ends, removing low-quality bases below a specified threshold (Q < 20 in this case).-	Sliding Window: this approach slides a 4-base window across the read and cuts when the average quality within the window drops below 20.-	Minlen: after trimming, reads shorter than 30 base pairs are discarded, as they are unlikely to map reliably.Post-trimming, a second round of FastQC confirmed significant improvement in base quality scores. For both samples, over 90% of the read pairs were retained after trimming. This step ensures that the downstream steps would not be hindered by poor quality, and unreliable reads.2.3	Alignment to the Human Reference Genome2.3.1 Reference Genome Retrieval and IndexingThe human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) was retrieved from the UCSC Genome Browser [3]. This version was the primary assembly, which includes non-canonical chromosomes such as alternative scaffolds and unlocalized contigs. This is not the ideal version for population-level analysis, where ‘analysis set’ is preferred. However, this issue was identified at a later stage, after substantial downstream processing has been completed. Given that the results of processing these two samples would not have impact on further analysis, the decision was made not to repeat the workflow with a different reference genome.To enable efficient alignment, the genome was indexed using BWA (Burrows-Wheeler Aligner) [4]. This constructs a full-text index in minute space using the Burrows-Wheeler Transform (BWT) and a backwards search algorithm. This indexing reduces memory usage and accelerates alignment.2.3.2 Read Alignment Using BWA-MEMSequencing reads were aligned to the indexed hg38 genome using the BWA-MEM algorithm, a method of the BWA tool for aligning short reads to a reference genome. BWA-MEM is well-suited for Illumina data due to its support of gapped alignments. It employs a seed-and-extend strategy:-	First it identifies maximal exact matches (MEMs) between the reads and the reference.-	Then, it chains these matches and performs Smith-Waterman alignment to fill gaps. This approach balances speed and accuracy while supporting paired-end information and alignment flags were included into the resulting SAM files.2.3.3 Conversion to BAM Format, Sorting, IndexingFollowing alignment, the resulting Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) files were converted to Binary Alignment Map (BAM) using SAMtools [5][6]. BAM is a compressed binary format that allows for efficient storage and retrieval of the alignments. The BAM files were then sorted by genomic coordinates and indexed for later variant calling analysis. Alignment statistics were generated to assess the quality of the mapping. Key metrics included total reads, mapped reads, percentage of properly paired reads, unmapped reads, and the mean insert size. Both samples exhibited high alignment rates (>99%), low error rates, and expected insert sizes, indicating successful and high-quality alignment.2.3.4 Addition of Read Group InformationA necessary requirement for variant calling using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) [7] is the presence of read group information in BAM files. Initially, this metadata was omitted during the BWA alignment, so it was added afterwards using GATK’s AddOrReplaceReadGroups utility. Read groups serve as identifiers that distinguish sequencing runs, platforms, and libraries. Each group contains fields such as RGID (read group ID), RGLB (library), RGPL (sequencing platfrom), RGPU (platform unit), RGSM (sample name). Adding this information ensures GATK can accurately handle multiple samples and libraries in joint variant calling procedures. After this, the BAM files were ready for variant discovery and genotype analysis.2.4	Variant discovery and AnnotationAll variant discovery steps were carried out following GATK Best Practices pipeline for germline short variant discovery [8][9]. This workflow is designed to ensure high-confidence variant calls by combining robust preprocessing, error modeling, and variant filtering approaches.2.4.1. Marking PCR and Optical DuplicatesAfter alignment, GATK’s MarkDuplicates was used to identify and mark duplicate reads arising from PCR amplification or optical artifacts during sequencing. These duplicates result from the same original DNA fragment being sequenced multiple times, rather than representing independent observations. If not accounted for, such duplicated can artificially inflate the false-positive values, or obscure true variants leading to high false-negative rates.This tool marks, but not removes duplicates allowing downstream tools (like variant callters) to disregard them. Both samples showed low to moderate duplication levels at 3.04% and 1.45% respectively. 2.4.2. Base Quality Score Recalibration (BQSR)The base quality scores estimate the probability of a sequencing error at each base. The accuracy of such scores is important for variant calling by weighing the evidence for and against possible alleles. However, systematic errors may arise from machine-specific biases, or context-dependent artifacts. To address this, GATK’s BaseRecalibrator was applied to recalibrate base quality scores based on empirical evidence. BQSR uses machine learning to detect systematic patterns in mismatches by comparing aligned reads to a database of known, high-quality variants. True polymorphisms should not be penalized, so the tool needs a set of known variants. Here, dbSNP build 151 [10] filtered for common variants was used to separate genuine variation from sequencing noise.Due to a mismatch in chromosome naming conventions between reference and target files (e.g. ‘chr1’ vs ‘1’), BCFtools annotate was used to harmonize chromosome names prior to BQSR. After this, the ApplyBQSR tool was used with the recalibration data table to edit the quality scores in the BAM file. This allows us to ensure the reliability of the variant confidence scores. 2.4.3. Variant Calling with HaplotypeCaller and Joint GenotypingFor variant discovery, HaplotypeCaller was used in GVCF mode. This tool first defines active regions – the ones with evidence for variation – and performing a de-novo assembly using a De Brujin-like graph. After that, the program uses a pair-hidden Markov model to compute likelihoods for each possible haplotype given the read data. The GVCF mode outputs an intermediate Genomic VCF file, with the same format specifications as a regular VCF, but containing extra information. The GVCF contains records for every genomic position, regardless of variant presence. This distinction is important for joint genotyping, where having genotype likelihoods for all position allows accurate comparisons across multiple samples. GenomicsDBImport creates a GenomicsDB workspace – a data structure developed by GATK collaborators for efficient access to genomic regions. GenotypeGVCFs uses this structure to produce a single multi-sample VCF file [11], calculating the genotype likelihoods.2.4.4. Variant Quality FiltrationWith only two samples, I could not apply Variant Quality Score Recalibration (VQSR), which requires a larger cohort. Instead, hard filtering was performed to exclude false positives. Variants were split by type, as SNPs and INDELs have different error profiles and require different filtering thresholds. SNP filtering criteria (based on GATK recommendations):-	QD (Quality by Depth) < 2.0-	FS (Fisher Strand Bias) > 60.0-	MQ (Mapping Quality) < 40.0-	MQRandSum < -12.5-	ReadPosRankSum < -8.0INDEL filtering criteria:-	QD < 2.0-	FS > 200.0-	ReadPosRankSum < -20.0Variants failing any criterion were flagged in the VCF file but not removed, allowing downstream tool to exclude them as needed. Finally , the filtered SNP and INDEL files were merged into a unified VCF.2.4.5. Variant AnnotationTo annotate the identified variants with known reference identifiers, ANNOVAR [12] was used to perform filter-based annotation. The avsnp150 dataset – an internally normalized version of dpSNP build 150 – was applied to match variants to ther corresponding rsIDs. This annotation enhances interpretability and enables efficient cross-referencing with public databases and existing literature. The output was retained in VCF format, with the original variant information preserved and supplemented with annotation fields. To keep track of references (format later):1.	Andrews, S. (2010). In this model, gene pools are simulated from a collection of geographically localized populations. The ancestry of the tested individuals is next estimated in relation to these gene pools. In this model, all individuals are represented as the proportion of gene pools. Their results do not change when samples are added or removed in the second part of the analysis. Population groups are bounded within the gene pools, and inclusion in these groups can be evaluated. This model was shown to be reliable, replicable, and accurate for many of the applications discussed here, including biogeography85, population structure modeling106, ancestry inference107, paleogenomic modeling108, forensics86, and cohort matching57. An evaluation of other tools that may be useful to infer the population structure and their limitations can be found elsewhere37,109.Overall critiques of pca‘There are no universal guidelines’First (?)
